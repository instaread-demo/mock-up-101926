Why Angola Prison In Louisiana Has Been Called The ‘Alcatraz Of The South’

By Rivy Lyon | Edited By Cara Johnson
Published October 18, 2026

The largest maximum-security penitentiary in the U.S., Angola Prison opened on the property of a former plantation in 1901 — and soon became notorious for its harsh conditions.

Angola Prison is the largest maximum-security penitentiary in the United States — and one of the most notorious. Officially known as the Louisiana State Penitentiary, the facility takes its nickname from a haunting source: the antebellum plantation that once stood on the property.

Angola Plantation was owned by Isaac Franklin, who enslaved roughly 100 laborers there. After the Civil War, it was purchased by former Confederate soldier Samuel Lawrence James, who used the convict lease system to farm the land.

This method proved so profitable that the property was transformed into a prison after James’ death. The inmates were in charge of harvesting cotton, herding cattle, and growing crops to sell in the surrounding community.

The practice continues to this day, and some prisoners make as little as two cents per hour for their labor. This, paired with “horrifying” conditions within the facility, has earned Angola Prison yet another nickname: “Alcatraz of the South.”

How A Louisiana Plantation Transformed Into A Maximum-Security Prison

Before the Civil War, the land where the Louisiana State Penitentiary stands today belonged to Isaac Franklin, a wealthy plantation owner who enslaved around 500 Africans on his properties along the Mississippi River.

Angola Plantation, a swampy forest where roughly 100 men and women lived and worked, was named for the African region of Angola where many enslaved laborers originated. Conditions there were brutal. Deadly illnesses frequently spread through the living quarters, and runaway attempts were common.

Louisiana State Penitentiary MuseumLouisiana State Penitentiary, commonly known as Angola Prison, occupies roughly 18,000 acres along the Mississippi River in Louisiana.

Upon Franklin’s death in 1846, his widow combined four of his plantations — including Angola — and sold them to Samuel Lawrence James, a former Confederate officer. To run his 18,000 acres of land, James leased convicts from the state of Louisiana — and worked many of them to death.

James died in 1894, and six years later, Louisiana purchased his property to build a penitentiary. The new state prison opened in 1901, and it soon became infamous for its horrendous conditions.

Life At Angola Prison, The ‘Alcatraz Of The South’

The harsh labor that had built Angola Plantation continued when the site became a state prison. Inmates spent their days farming, herding cattle, and picking cotton. In their 1992 book The Life and Legend of Leadbelly — the biography of a folk singer who was once imprisoned at Angola — Charles Wolfe and Kip Lornell described the penitentiary as “probably as close to slavery as any person could come in 1930.”

Library of CongressHuddie Ledbetter, the folk singer known as Leadbelly (pictured here in the foreground), was imprisoned at Angola for attempted homicide in the 1930s.

In 1951, 55 inmates at Angola Prison cut their own Achilles tendons to protest against “brutality” at the facility. They were dubbed the Heel String Gang because they hopped around on one foot and sang a tune they called “The Heel-String Boogie.” As the Alexandria Daily Town Talk reported that May, the prison responded by considering “the abolition of corporal punishment.”

Still, Angola was considered the “worst prison in America” throughout the 1950s, and the American Bar Association reportedly referred to the penitentiary as “medieval, squalid and horrifying” in the 1970s.

One of the penitentiary’s worst areas was the Red Hat Cell Block, a solitary confinement unit. Inmates there had to wear distinctive red hats while working in the fields. Before it closed in the 1970s, per Mother Jones, men who had been incarcerated in the unit described “a dungeon crawling with rats, where dinner was served in stinking buckets splashed onto the floors.”

Public DomainInmates bunking in the hallway of Angola Prison due to overcrowding. 1975.

Reform efforts in recent decades have made conditions at Angola Prison slightly better — but it remains a grim place to this day.

Angola Prison In The Modern Era

In many ways, the Louisiana State Penitentiary still holds onto its plantation past. It operates as a working farm, and new inmates are often assigned to the cotton fields to labor in the brutal heat of the Deep South while guards patrol on horseback. They reportedly face punishment if they refuse.

According to PBS, prisoners sued the penitentiary in September 2023 to end this practice during the Louisiana summers. “I saw guys collapse,” one inmate told reporters. “There were dudes that got heat stroke. There were dudes with underlying conditions, older or had some sort of disability, but they had to go out there, too.”

The Atlantic/YouTubeA correctional officer on horseback oversees Angola Prison inmates as they work in the fields in 2015.

Other prisoners farm corn and soybeans that are then sold on the open market. There are also several manufacturing facilities that are staffed by inmates. The prison reportedly brings in millions of dollars each year from these endeavors — while the inmates make as little as two cents per hour for their work.

As such, the debate surrounding Angola and its prison labor system is heated. Some people believe that the work is appropriate due to the nature of some inmates’ crimes. In 2016, 71 percent of those incarcerated at the Louisiana State Penitentiary were serving life sentences.

Notable former prisoners include Derrick Todd Lee, the Baton Rouge serial killer who murdered seven women. Ronald Dominique, the “Bayou Strangler” behind the deaths of dozens of homeless men, is still incarcerated there.

However, opponents of the system argue that this forced labor is a form of cruel and unusual punishment. And perhaps no aspect of life at Angola sparks as much criticism as the Angola Prison Rodeo.

The event has been held each April and October since the 1960s, and it has become so popular that the penitentiary built a 10,000-person stadium in 2000. It features horses and cattle raised at the facility — and the inmates are part of the entertainment. Some volunteer for “Convict Poker,” in which four men play cards in the arena while an angry bull runs at them. Critics call it inhumane, but not all of the prisoners agree.

Angola Prison Rodeo/Facebook“Convict Poker” at the Angola Prison Rodeo.

Donald Cook, an inmate who has been imprisoned at Angola for murder since 1991, signs up for the rodeo each year. He once suffered seven broken ribs and a collapsed lung when he was struck by a bull, but he told Mississippi Public Broadcasting in 2025, “The rodeo has showed me a purpose and a life in here.”

Throughout the rest of the year, the prison offers several other opportunities for inmates to fulfill their passions. The institution produces a magazine called The Angolite that is written and edited by prisoners and available to the general public. It also hosts the country’s only FCC-licensed radio station that is operated by inmates.

Under Warden Burl Cain, who served at Angola from 1995 to 2016, the rate of violent crimes and murders within the walls of the penitentiary fell sharply. Still, Angola Prison maintains its vicious reputation.

Shortly before Hurricane Gustav made landfall in Louisiana in September 2008, as reported by CBS News at the time, New Orleans Mayor Ray Nagin announced at a press conference, “We will have zero tolerance for looters. You will go directly to Angola Prison, and God bless you when you go there.”
